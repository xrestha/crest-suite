### S549 — 2026-08-12 — `/impeccable critique` phases 1–2: the app shell and every pre-auth surface, measured rather than eyeballed

A phased critique-and-fix pass over the whole product, two isolated agents per phase (a design review driving the live app, and a deterministic detector + measured browser evidence pass), synthesised and then fixed. Phases 1 and 2 are done — the shell plus shared components, and the public/entry surfaces. Everything below was measured in the running app against a real Pro client, not read off the JSX.

**The largest finding was invisible on the shipped theme.** One agent measured the sidebar on Dark and correctly called its contrast strong (6.05–8.27:1 across every text tier); the other measured the *same sidebar on Latte* and found **52 of 56 text elements below WCAG AA**. Both were right — nobody had ever measured a light preset. Five of the ten shipped presets are light.

The proximate cause was `subscription.js` returning hardcoded Dark-preset hex (`#34d399`/`#fbbf24`/`#f87171`/`#c9a84c`, plus a `#4b5563` documented nowhere), so the "10 months left" badge painted Dark's green on Latte's `#e6e9ef` at **1.58:1**. Swapping those to tokens exposed the real problem underneath: **the presets themselves fail.** Measured across all five light presets, **23 of 25 signal-colour/surface combinations were under 4.5:1** — Latte's amber at 2.15, Rosé Dawn's at 1.87, its accent at 2.37 — and `--theme-text3` failed on **all five** (3.72–4.02).

**The fix keeps the palettes and splits the role.** Latte, Rosé Dawn and Solarized are faithful reproductions of palettes people choose *because* they recognise those exact values, and the same tokens paint charts and tints where the lighter value is correct. So each light preset now additionally declares `greenText`/`redText`/`amberText`/`purpleText`/`accentInk` — darkened, hue-preserving, clearing 4.5:1 against that preset's worst surface (its own sidebar). Dark presets declare none; `applyTheme` falls back to the base colour, so nothing changes there. This mirrors the `accent`/`accentText` pairing that already existed — note `accentInk` is *not* `accentText`: the first is the accent used **as** text, the second is the foreground that sits **on** an accent fill.

The neutral ramp was corrected in place instead, because those tokens are only ever text — there is no fill use to preserve. Targets keep three distinct tiers (~6.5 / ~5.6 / ~4.6) rather than flattening everything onto the 4.5 floor. Sidebar on Latte went **52 failures → 0** across 71 elements; `.badge-*` in `Layout.css` now points at the text variants, so most status text in the product inherited the fix without a per-page edit.

**Poppins reached no form control anywhere in the app.** `body` sets it, but browsers do not inherit `font-family` into inputs and buttons — so all 14 controls on the login page, all 13 pricing CTAs and **every `.btn` in the product** rendered in Arial. `Layout.css` had been patching this one class at a time (10 `font-family: inherit` declarations, none of them `.btn` or `.form-field input`), which is the tell that inheritance was always the intent. One rule in `index.css` replaces the whole strategy. It is the most visually noticeable change in the session and it touches every screen.

**Shell fixes** (each multiplies across all 86 routes):

- **41 focusable controls preceded `<main>` on every route, with no skip link** — WCAG 2.4.1 Level A. Added, plus three labelled landmarks where there were two unlabelled ones; the module switcher and bottom rail previously sat in **no** landmark at all.
- **The sidebar clipped text in three places.** The client's white-labelled wordmark rendered **"BHATT / CHOIL"**, sliced mid-glyph, as the first element on every screen — it now uses two lines and shows the name in full. The owner's own three destinations rendered "Ow… / Re… / Da…" (measured: 27px label boxes against 39/39/68px of text) and are now full-width rows. The collapsed 56px rail rendered "IM / HR / PO", **contradicting DESIGN.md's own Navigation spec**, which has always claimed the switcher goes icon-only.
- **The mobile drawer was a modal with none of a modal's behaviour** — Escape did nothing, focus never entered it, the hamburger's accessible name was the literal character `☰` at 36×36, and it opened at whatever scroll position the nav was last left at (measured 642px in, landing mid-list on the accountant's reports). All fixed; 44×44.
- **The `@media (pointer: coarse)` rule reached not one shell control.** It covered `.btn`/`.btn-sm`/`.panel-tab` only, so on a tablet the entire navigation stayed desktop-density — nav links 37px, module tabs 35px, the calculator trigger 27×25. S546 brought touch sizing to the authenticated app and skipped the navigation.
- **Ctrl-K had no dialog contract and its focus trap leaked** — two Tab presses put focus on `<body>` with the overlay still up, and because `handleKeyDown` was bound to the input alone, **Escape stopped working the moment focus left it**, while the UI kept showing an "Esc" hint. Now a real dialog with combobox/listbox/option ARIA, a live region, focus restored to the trigger, and the silent 30-result cap made visible. `item.groupLabel` had been rendered by the component since it was written and **never set by `Layout.js`** — now wired, so searching "report" no longer returns four identically-named destinations from three modules with nothing to tell them apart.
- **`pulse-dot`, the shell's only infinite animation, was an inline `style={{ animation }}`** — unreachable by `prefers-reduced-motion`, the exact failure class DESIGN.md elevated to a Named Rule after fixing it in `ChartCard`. Moved to a class; the keyframe now uses `currentColor` (an amber dot had been pulsing inside a hardcoded red halo); and the guard now covers the two live collapse transitions instead of `.sidebar-toggle`, a class with **zero JSX references**.
- Pins were mouse-only on all 65 rows — the shell's own answer to a 41-item nav, unavailable without a mouse. Now `role="button"` + `tabIndex` + `aria-pressed`, revealed on `:focus-within`.

The shell had never stated any of it: client and plan lived only inside `.sidebar-content` (`display:none` at 56px, behind a drawer on mobile), and the period was printed by whichever page happened to print it. Every IMS figure is period-scoped, an admin can be viewing as another tenant, and an Owner can now switch outlets — three independent ways to read a real number off the wrong books.

Also: 18 off-scale radii onto the token scale, a `<main>` landmark added (the page had only a `<nav>`), and the trial length — stated four different ways, including an FAQ asking *"Is the 1-month trial really free?"* and answering "7 days" on a page headlined "Simple, honest pricing" — collapsed to one `TRIAL_DAYS` constant.

- **`SubscriptionLock` told a locked-out paying customer to "get in touch" and gave no way to.** All three copy variants end that way; the component imported `useAuth` only, while `contact_phone`/`contact_email` sit in settings and are already rendered by `Help.js` — a page behind the lock. This is a recovery flow, so the user is there *because* something already went wrong. 8s timeout into a real error with an exit. Its card also overrode `.login-right`'s padding to `0`, putting the heading 1px from the edge and physically inside the 24px corner radius.

A 2026-07-14 note argued that demoting the fills satisfied the One Accent Rule, but that rule is not "one solid fill", it is "Aged Brass is the only non-semantic colour on any screen". Green means paid/approved/healthy everywhere else and was being spent on navigation; purple is the *rationed* categorical token and "Staff Login" is not a fourth category of anything. Both also measured 2.75–3.34:1 on the light presets. Now one hue plus neutrals. The trial block also gained a real `<h2>` (the page's only heading had been "Welcome back", so a form that creates a live client record was indistinguishable from the sign-in form in the heading outline) and its fields were retitled "Business email" / "Create a password", since the sign-in form 300px above used identical labels and the same placeholder for the opposite consequence.

And `Pricing`'s five "Get X →" buttons still route to `/login`, discarding the plan the buyer just chose. ### S548 — 2026-08-12 — Retiered every IMS feature by attribute, collapsed Suite into one add-on, and built Multi-Outlet

Started as "reanalyse the plans & pricing — the Suite has nothing other than 2 features." It turned out the tier structure had never been *designed*; it had accreted, and reading it against the code found three separate problems.

**Two Starter features could not produce a number on Starter data.** `ReorderReport.js` imports `explodeRecipeIngredients` and derives consumption by exploding sold recipes into ingredients; `StockMovements.js` is built entirely on recipe explosion plus `subRecipeUsage`, and even maintains a `noBomRecipes` gap list. Both sat in `STARTER_KEYS` while `recipe_costing` is Growth-gated — so a Starter client was sold a Reorder Report with no consumption figure and a stock ledger that was permanently empty. Neither fails loudly; they just render nothing, forever.

**The placement framework that came out of it**, which every future feature should be scored against — five attributes (persona altitude, prerequisite depth, frequency, value type, substitutability), with the tier thesis falling out of the fourth: **Starter = Record & Comply, Growth = Control, Pro = Strategy, Crest Suite Pro = Synthesis.** Two standing rules follow. *A statutory obligation never gates above the base tier* — the codebase already settled this, since `vat_report`/`non_vat_report` were always Starter, but `vendor_balance_confirmation` (IRD Annexure 13) had been sitting at Pro. And *a data-entry page must not sit above the tier of any figure that consumes it* — `overheads` was Pro while `ClientDashboard`, `OwnerDashboard`, `computeMonthlyReport` and `Recipes`' True Cost all read what it writes.

Seven moves, applied in both `AuthContext.js`'s key sets **and** the `minPlan` props in `App.js` — those are two independent mechanisms, and nav visibility runs off a third (`isItemVisible` calls `hasFeature`, not `minPlan`), so all three had to agree or a page ends up reachable-but-hidden:

| Feature | Was | Now | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `reorder_report` | Starter | **Growth** | Consumption comes from recipe explosion |
| `stock_movement_log` | Starter | **Growth** | Same — empty without a BOM |
| `outstanding_payables` | Growth | **Starter** | Record-keeping; needs no recipes |
| `vendor_balance_confirmation` | Pro | **Starter** | Statutory, same class as VAT |
| `overheads` | Pro | **Growth** | Feeds Fixed Costs %/Net Margin |
| `demand_forecast` | Pro | **Suite** | Cross-module — `Roster.jsx` overlays it on the HR roster |
| `fixed_asset_register` | Pro | **Suite** | Owner/finance altitude, self-contained |

Starter's count is unchanged (13 in, 13 out) and every remaining Starter feature now produces output. Pro drops 12 → 7, which is the honest consequence of the altitude rule and the reason Pro's price should not rise while Suite absorbs the strategic surface.

**`guest_ordering` was a POS feature gated on the IMS plan** — `FeatureAccessModal` already marked it `planSource: 'pos'` while `AuthContext` had it in `PRO_KEYS`, and `hasFeature()` resolves `plan` from the IMS tier. A POS client on IMS Starter could not buy it at any price. It is now in a `POS_MODULE_KEYS` set that unlocks with the module, since POS is deliberately flat.

**Crest Suite collapsed from three hollow tiers to one add-on SKU.** Both `SuiteGate` call sites were `minTier="growth"`, so **Suite Starter (NPR 5,300/mo) unlocked nothing at all** and Suite Pro added nothing over Suite Growth on its own axis — the only delta was the bundled IMS tier. `Pricing.js` sold the bundle with a strikethrough price and **no feature list whatsoever**. It is now one SKU at +NPR 2,000/mo **per outlet, on top of** a client's modules rather than containing them, with seven features. That inverted two things in `ClientDrawer.js` that were correct for a bundle and destructive for an add-on: a "reconciliation" effect that force-enabled all three modules and overwrote `plan`/`hr_plan`/`pos_plan` to match `suite_plan` the moment an admin opened the drawer (deleted), and the module toggles/pricing locked behind a "Controlled by Suite Bundle" badge (unlocked). `clientMRR()` in `AdminDashboardOverview.jsx` now *adds* Suite instead of returning early and ignoring the modules entirely.

**Nobody loses a feature, and the mechanism already existed.** `hasFeature()` is "plan tier **OR** explicit flag", and `SuiteGate`'s own `overridden` branch reads `hasFeature()` — so grandfathering is a `feature_flags` sweep, no code. `20260812160000` sets `reorder_report`/`stock_movement_log` for every Starter client and `demand_forecast`/`fixed_asset_register` for every Pro client, including an INSERT for clients with no `feature_flags` row at all, who would otherwise fall through both UPDATEs silently. `suite_plan='growth'` sweeps to `'pro'` (2 features → 5, a pure upgrade); **`'starter'` is deliberately left alone** — those clients paid for a tier that unlocked nothing *and* the bundle implied their module subscriptions, so both halves are a per-client billing conversation, not a blanket UPDATE.

**Multi-Outlet (`20260812170000`) — the flagship Suite feature, built on selected-outlet indirection rather than policy rewriting.** `my_client_id()` appears in **151 places across 18 migrations**. Rewriting them to a set-returning `my_client_ids()` would touch every policy on ~50 tables and permanently widen RLS from "one client" to "any client in my group", removing RLS as the backstop behind `scopedDb`'s filter. Instead: add `profiles.active_client_id` and make `my_client_id()` a `coalesce(active_client_id, client_id)`. Every policy keeps its exact shape. The frontend gets it free — `useScopedDb` binds `clientId` straight from `AuthContext`, so one value re-scopes ~200 call sites and all 65 `CLIENT_SCOPED_TABLES` with no per-page edit.

Both columns default NULL, so the `coalesce` returns `client_id` and **every ungrouped client is byte-identical to before**. No policy is rewritten, so all 151 sites keep their *semantics*, not merely their current results.

Four things that shaped it:

- **`active_client_id` is never user-writable.** It decides which tenant every RLS policy resolves to, so it is privilege-bearing and deliberately **not** added to `guard_profiles_privileged_columns()`'s allow-list (S531 invariant #1). `set_active_outlet()` is the only write path; under `SECURITY DEFINER` `current_user` is the owner, so the guard passes it through — the same mechanism `record_pos_pin_attempt` relies on. Membership is validated at **write** time so `my_client_id()` stays a join-free `coalesce` (it runs per row across ~120 policies), and a trigger on `clients.group_id` clears stale selections.
- **`clients_select` is the one policy that had to change** — it was `id = my_client_id() OR is_admin()`, so a customer could not read that a sibling outlet existed at all.
- **`get_group_summary()` filters to `suite_plan = 'pro'` server-side and returns the excluded outlets by name.** A client-side filter would ship an unpaid outlet's revenue to the browser and then hide it. It returns raw aggregates rather than percentages, so the page does not become a fourth independent definition of food cost % / labour cost %.
- **`pos_orders` has no `period_id` or BS columns — only AD `closed_at`** — and BS→AD conversion lives in JS, not SQL. The RPC takes `p_ad_start`/`p_ad_end` and the page converts with `bsToAd()`, matching `SalesReport.jsx`'s convention. The first draft got this wrong by deriving a range from `min(created_at)` on the period row, which is when the period was *created*, not the month it covers.

Three edge cases handled rather than discovered: a lapsed outlet inside a healthy group locks only itself (`getAccessState`/`ProtectedRoute` are per selected outlet), so the switcher badges it; outlets keep independent periods (`monthly_periods` is `UNIQUE(client_id, bs_year, bs_month)`), so the console aligns on `(bs_year, bs_month)` and names outlets with no period rather than showing zero; and switching is **blocked while the offline queue is non-empty** — checking both `getQueue()` and `getPosOrderQueue()`, since stock ops write against the current tenant just as POS orders do.

**HQ→branch master-data push was deliberately left out.** It is the only destructive operation in the design, and deferring costs nothing — the lineage column it needs is purely additive with no backfill. It also deserves customer validation first: head-office menu enforcement suits chains running one identical menu, while independent Nepali groups often run genuinely different menus per location. When picked up, note that `items`/`recipes` have **no** `UNIQUE(client_id, name)` (only `categories` does), so it must match on an added `master_id`, never by name.

**Smoke-testing the retier surfaced a revenue leak that predates it: `hr_plan`/`pos_plan` silently raise the IMS tier.** `plan` in `AuthContext.js` is not `clients.plan` — it is the **maximum** across `clients.plan`, `ims_plan`, `hr_plan`, `pos_plan` and `is_premium` (which forces `'pro'` outright). Found live on a test client sitting at `clients.plan = 'starter'` with `hr_plan`/`pos_plan` both `'pro'`: it resolved to Pro and got every IMS Pro feature, having bought none of them. Since HR and POS are sold flat and nothing in the product charges for those columns, **any client with either module enabled and its plan column at `'pro'` is getting IMS Pro free.** This was coherent while Suite was a bundle — `handleSuitePlanPick` deliberately set all three columns together, which is exactly the code deleted this session — and is a straight leak under the add-on model. The README already contradicted itself here: one entry calls `hr_plan`/`pos_plan` "harmless unused strings", a later one documents `plan` as deriving "the highest tier across all enabled module plans". **Fixed, and the concept removed with it.** `plan` now reads `clients.plan` (plus the legacy `is_premium` boolean, which at least means what it says) and nothing else — verified live on a client left at `plan='starter'` with both module columns still `'pro'`, which now correctly resolves to Starter. `c?.ims_plan` went too: that column has **never existed** (`42703` against the live DB), so it was always `undefined` and silently dropped by `filter(Boolean)` — this README's own Module-flags line still lists it as real.

**HR and POS are yes/no modules, so the tier concept was pulled out of them everywhere, not just out of the plan max.** `pos_plan` turned out to gate something real despite a code comment claiming it was unwired: `PosOrders.jsx` laddered the upsell-suggestion engine off it (category nudge at 'starter', co-occurrence at 'growth', Menu Engineering filter at 'pro'), and since `ClientDrawer` defaulted the column to `'starter'` and wrote it on every save, most POS clients had been getting only the basic nudge. Buying POS now buys the whole engine; the genuine IMS dependency stays, because co-occurrence is sold as the IMS+POS combination and the ME filter needs `recipes.me_class`, which only IMS populates. The category nudge survives as a real fallback for POS-only clients rather than a tier artifact — `rank()` filters to `_score > 0`, so without it a client with no IMS and no manual pairings would have seen an empty panel. Also removed: the `hr_plan`/`pos_plan` writes in `ClientDrawer`'s save, the tier suffix on the HR/POS admin pills (now "HR · on"/"POS · off"), the "HR: pro"/"POS: pro" tags on the admin dashboard, the `pos_plan` fallbacks in `FeatureAccessModal`, and both columns from the two `select()` lists that fetched them. The columns stay in the DB, vestigial and unread.

**The sign-in render loop was a ping-pong between two guards, and it was pre-existing** (confirmed by stashing every change from this session and reproducing it). `ProtectedRoute`'s `if (!profile) -> /login` raced `Login`'s `if (ready && session) -> /dashboard`: on a fresh sign-in `session` is set the instant SIGNED_IN fires while `profile` is still null, because `fetchProfile` is deliberately not awaited there (the GoTrue deadlock fix). Each guard sent the user straight back to the other until the profile landed. Fixed on both sides so either alone breaks the cycle — `fetchProfile` now sets `loading` true up front and `ProtectedRoute` waits on it rather than concluding the user is signed out, and `Login`'s redirect additionally requires `profile`, so a session whose profile fetch genuinely failed lands on the sign-in form instead of looping.

**One loop in this session's own code, caught only by logging in as a real client.** `setOutlets([])` in `fetchProfile` returned a fresh array on every call; React bails out of a re-render only when `Object.is(prev, next)` holds, so "set it to empty again" is a state *change*, and a new identity into the context value on every profile fetch cascaded until React gave up. It fires for non-admin accounts only, which is why an admin-session smoke test came back clean. `nextOutletsOrSame()` now returns the previous array when the outlet list has not changed — the ungrouped case being both the common one and the cheap path. Verified by bisect: baseline 0 errors on reload, 3 with the bug, 0 after the fix.

**Tier names were left as Starter/Growth/Pro.** Bud/Bloom/Harvest and several other sets were explored and dropped — the naming research is clear that tier names should rank themselves and that "overly creative" ones measurably hurt conversion, and renaming would have touched marketing copy, the Guide's ~30 `plan:` strings and how existing clients describe themselves, for no structural gain. Only *which features sit in each tier* moved.

### S545 — 2026-08-12 — Export / Import: a backup that can actually restore, and an Archive that can be undone

Follow-on from S544. Asking what `trial_purge_at` would do if it were made real surfaced that the codebase had **no export of a client's data at all**, and three destructive buttons that were already live, already irreversible, and had no undo.

**What was built.** `exportClientData.js` walks ~65 tables — `CLIENT_SCOPED_TABLES` from `scopedDb.js` (imported, not re-enumerated), the period-scoped seven, and four parent-scoped children — and emits **two artifacts**: an `.xlsx` workbook per table for a human, and a `.json` that `restoreClientData.js` can actually put back. Shipping only the spreadsheet would have produced something that looks like a backup and cannot restore: `monthly_owner_reports.snapshot` is `jsonb` and renders as `[object Object]` in a cell, and nulls/numbers/dates do not round-trip faithfully through a sheet.

**Every read raises `fetchAllRows`' cap to 500k.** The default is 100,000, and `pos_order_items` is one row per line per bill — so a busy client would have produced a **silently short backup**, the S528 failure mode in the one place it would be least survivable.

**Three things the research settled that inspection would have got wrong:**

- **A browser genuinely can write to `C:\CrestBackups\<Client>\`** — but only through the File System Access API, and only durably for an **installed PWA**: Chrome 122+ auto-persists the permission for installed apps and skips the three-way prompt entirely. Crest already ships `display: standalone`, so installing it to the desktop is what makes the unattended path work; in a plain tab expect a prompt per session. Firefox/Safari fall back to an ordinary download.
- **Every client-scoped table has an `id`** — verified by regex over the migrations rather than assumed, because `readAll` orders by it and `staff_pin_vault` (PK `user_id`) turned out to be the sole exception. It is read on a separate path, so it was never affected.
- **The PIN vault belongs in the backup after all.** The first design excluded it on the grounds that PINs must not sit on a local disk. That was right about plaintext and wrong about ciphertext: `staff_pin_vault` rows are AES-GCM encrypted under `app_secrets.pin_vault_key`, which stays in the database and never enters the file. The backup is inert to anyone who finds it — and it is what lets POS/Self-Service staff keep their **original PINs** through a restore.

**Attribution survives a delete, via the project's own S435 rule.** Every `*_by`/`custodian_user_id` UUID gets a `*_by_name` resolved through `get_client_profile_names()` **at export time**. On restore the UUIDs are nulled — they reference `profiles` rows a full delete already removed, and restoring them verbatim raises FK violations across most tables — but "who did it" is still readable in the artifact forever. Nulling is safe on S543's own evidence: a grep proved no `*_by` column is ever filtered on.

**Archive Client is the real answer to churn, and it cost almost nothing.** It composes three existing things: a backup, the existing `deleteClientData` (which already keeps the client row, settings, flags, profiles and every auth login), and `is_active = false`. That third step only became meaningful in S544, where `is_active` stopped being a badge colour and started locking the app. So the common case — a client leaves — is now **fully reversible including logins**, and the partial path stops being the default. `Delete Client` remains for genuine erasure, with its limitation stated in the UI rather than discovered mid-recovery.

**Restore after a full delete is deliberately partial, and says so.** PIN accounts come back exactly: the generated `*@pos.internal` email is in the backup, the PIN is in the vault, and the same email + same PIN yields the same derived password, so staff sign in unchanged (`restore_staff_accounts`, admin-only). Password accounts — IMS staff, HR staff, Owner — come back as a **named to-recreate list**, because their login is a real human email the export deliberately does not carry and their passwords are user-chosen and never vaulted (S539). Re-provisioning only runs when the target has no logins, so restoring an *archived* client never creates a duplicate set.

**The pre-flight backup blocks by default and never blocks absolutely.** All four destructive actions export first and abort if it fails. A "I have backed up elsewhere" checkbox exists because without it Danger Zone would be unreachable on any browser lacking the File System Access API — a worse outcome than the risk being guarded.

### S544 — 2026-08-12 — A lapsed subscription now actually locks the app

Started as a question about one client on Admin → Clients — a trial account showing **Inactive · 4d left**, not renewing. The answer was that *nothing would happen*, and that turned out to be the finding.

**`clients.is_active` enforced nothing whatsoever.** The Activate/Deactivate button on the client card writes the column ([`AdminClients.js:162`](src/pages/AdminClients.js#L162)) and that is the end of it. Grepped project-wide: the column is read in exactly two places — the badge and toggle on that card, and a count on `AdminDashboardOverview.jsx`. It appears in **no RLS policy**, no route guard, and nowhere in `AuthContext`/`Login`/`ProtectedRoute`. A client marked Inactive could log in and use the full product, unchanged, forever. The same held for every `*_ends_at` column: the only code that reads them is the auto-sweep in `loadClients()`, which sets… `is_active = false`. A cosmetic flag setting another cosmetic flag.

The trial half had the same shape from the other direction. `trialExpired` was computed properly in `AuthContext` but consumed **only** by two banners in `Layout.js` — a client whose 7-day trial ended read "🔒 Trial ended" at the top of a fully working app. And `trial_purge_at`, set to +22d at signup and rendered as a retention countdown in two places, has **no consumer at all** — no cron, no Edge Function, nothing that deletes anything. It was a countdown to an event that does not exist.

**Enforcement goes at one choke point, not on pages.** Every in-app route — IMS, HR, POS alike — mounts through the single `<ProtectedRoute><Layout /></ProtectedRoute>` in `App.js`, so that is where the lock lives. A per-page check would reopen the entire product the first time someone adds a route and forgets the guard, which is precisely how `is_active` came to mean nothing.

`getAccessState(client)` in `src/utils/subscription.js` is the one place the decision is made, sharing the existing "farthest end date across all modules" logic with `getSubStatus`. It **fails open** on purpose: a client with no end date on any module has never been given one — most of the existing book predates per-module dates — and must keep working. Only a date that exists *and* has passed locks anything.

**A 7-day grace period, and the reason for it.** Cutting a restaurant off at midnight on the due date strands a service in progress, and a lapsed invoice here is usually a collection delay rather than a decision to leave. So expiry first shows a red countdown banner naming the day access stops, and only then does `SubscriptionLock` replace the app. Trials get no grace — their expiry date *is* the decision point, and the retention window that follows is about keeping the data, not about continued access. An explicit admin deactivation outranks every date and locks immediately; that switch now means something.

Two follow-through details rather than loose ends: `AuthContext`'s client query was missing both `is_active` and `suite_ends_at` (so the Suite bundle's own expiry was invisible to the app), and the two post-expiry trial banners in `Layout.js` became **unreachable** the moment the lock shipped — a trial-expired client never renders `Layout` now. Removed rather than left looking live; their copy, including the retention countdown, moved into `SubscriptionLock`'s trial case.

**What this is not: a security boundary.** This is a UI gate. RLS still permits a locked client's JWT to read and write its own rows, so anyone willing to call PostgREST directly is unaffected. Real enforcement means a `client_id`-scoped expiry check inside the RESTRICTIVE policy families on ~50 tables — a much larger and riskier change, deliberately not attempted here. For the actual business case (a restaurant that stops paying) the UI gate is the whole product; for a determined ex-customer it is not.

**The grace period was defeated by the admin page, and nearly shipped that way.** `loadClients()` in `AdminClients.js` auto-deactivates any client whose module dates have all passed — at the raw expiry date, with no grace — and it runs on every visit to Admin → Clients. Since `is_active = false` is an immediate lock, a client one day past expiry would keep their countdown banner only until an admin happened to open that page, then be cut off early at a moment having nothing to do with them. The sweep now measures against `now − GRACE_DAYS`, so auto-deactivation and the lock screen agree on one date. Admin's own Deactivate button is untouched — a deliberate act still locks at once. Caught by asking what happens to one specific client four days out, not by reading the diff.

**On deploy this takes effect immediately and retroactively** — any client already toggled Inactive, or lapsed more than 7 days, is locked on their next page load. Review Admin → Clients before shipping.

### S542 — 2026-08-12 — Performance Advisor: 41 per-row `auth.uid()` calls and three stacked generations of RLS policy

Supabase's Performance Advisor was reporting two lint families across the whole schema — `auth_rls_initplan` on **41 policies** and `multiple_permissive_policies` on **15 tables**. Both now read zero.

**No DB password on this machine**, so live state was reconstructed by replaying `supabase/migrations/` in filename order (160 policies, tracking CREATE/DROP per `table||name`). That reconstruction independently reproduced both advisor lists exactly — same 41 policies, same 15 tables — which is what made it trustworthy enough to write RLS changes against. A useful confirmation fell out of it: the policies the linter *didn't* flag are precisely the ones using the `my_client_id()` helper rather than an inline `auth.uid()`.

**`multiple_permissive_policies` — three generations of the same rule, all live at once.** Postgres ORs together every permissive policy matching a (role, command) pair and executes all of them per row. Ten tables carried `admin_all_<x>` (`TO authenticated`, `is_admin()`), a broad `<x>_all`, *and* per-command `<x>_select/insert/update/delete` repeating `<x>_all` four ways. `admin_all_<x>` is a strict subset of the other two — every row it admits, they admit — so it was pure overhead. Keeping `<x>_all` alone preserves the grant exactly. Two tables needed more than a drop:

- **`hr_employees_update` was written with no `FOR` clause**, so it is `FOR ALL`, not `FOR UPDATE` — which is why it overlapped its three per-command siblings on SELECT/INSERT/DELETE. All four carry an identical predicate, so scoping it to UPDATE removes the overlap without changing who can do what. A policy's command cannot be changed by `ALTER POLICY`, so this one is a drop + recreate.
- **`vendor_returns`** had an admin `FOR ALL` policy overlapping four client per-command policies. The client policies don't include admin, so the admin one couldn't simply be dropped; all five collapse into a single `vendor_returns_all` in the same shape every other `client_id`-scoped table already uses.

Equivalence on the period-scoped tables was worth checking rather than assuming, since the two forms are written differently: `<x>_all` uses `EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM monthly_periods p WHERE p.id = period_id AND …)` while the per-command policies use `(SELECT client_id FROM monthly_periods WHERE id = period_id) = my_client_id()`. For a missing or NULL `period_id` the scalar form yields NULL (falsy in a policy) and the EXISTS form yields false — identical admitted row sets.

**`auth_rls_initplan` — 36 statements, not 41**, because the consolidation removes five of the flagged policies outright. `ALTER POLICY` rather than drop + recreate on purpose: it changes only the expression and leaves the command and role list untouched, so no table ever sits with a policy missing and there's no chance of fumbling a `FOR`/`TO` clause. Where a policy has USING only (a `FOR ALL` with no explicit WITH CHECK, or a SELECT/DELETE policy) only USING is restated — an omitted clause is left as-is by `ALTER POLICY`, which is also what keeps a `FOR SELECT` policy from being handed an illegal WITH CHECK.

**The bigger cost is one the linter cannot see.** It only pattern-matches `auth.<fn>()` and `current_setting()`, so it says nothing about `is_admin()` / `my_client_id()` / `is_hr_self_service()` — which appear in roughly **120 policies** and are the larger per-row expense. All three are `LANGUAGE sql STABLE SECURITY DEFINER`, and **Postgres refuses to inline a SECURITY DEFINER SQL function** (`inline_function()` bails on `prosecdef`), so each is a genuine function call per row rather than a folded-in subquery the planner can hoist. Wrapping them as `(select public.is_admin())` would hoist them exactly the way this pass hoists `auth.uid()`. Deliberately scoped out — far wider than the advisor asked for, and it wants a live `EXPLAIN` to confirm the win first. The two policies recreated from scratch here already use the wrapped form.

### S539 — 2026-08-12 — The PIN vault: making the pepper survivable, and admin-recoverable staff PINs

S538 left an operational cost that got rejected on sight: **`PIN_PEPPER` was neither recoverable nor rotatable.** PINs were stored nowhere, so losing the pepper meant hand-resetting every POS and Self-Service account across every client — "too exhausting for any admin," which is fair. The ask was a mechanism where only the admin can view a PIN.

**First finding, from reading the UI rather than assuming: managers already type PINs themselves.** `PosStaff.jsx` is a plain `<input>` at both create and reset, never a generator — so "view the PIN when created" already existed. The real gap was looking one up *later*, which is what got built.

**The design turn that made it work: a vault encrypted with a key you must not lose merely renames the problem.** Protecting the vault with a `PIN_VAULT_KEY` env secret would have traded "don't lose `PIN_PEPPER`" for "don't lose `PIN_VAULT_KEY`" at zero net gain. So **both secrets moved into the database** — `app_secrets`, an admin-only singleton whose two columns Postgres generates itself (`encode(gen_random_bytes(32),'base64')`). No `supabase secrets set`, nothing to put in a password manager, covered by the normal Supabase backups. This does not weaken the pepper against the attack it exists to stop: that attacker holds only the anon key, and `app_secrets` has no `anon` grant, so the pepper stays uncomputable off-server and direct `/token` brute force remains impossible. It is reachable only by someone who already holds an admin session or the service-role key — who could call `reset_pos_pin` anyway.

**`staff_pin_vault` stores each PIN AES-GCM-encrypted (WebCrypto, random 12-byte IV prepended, never pgcrypto — so the key never passes through SQL where it could land in a query log).** Its *primary* job is disaster recovery, not lookup: `rederive_pin_passwords` optionally rotates the pepper, then walks the vault re-deriving and rewriting every account's password. **That is the only reason the pepper is now rotatable in place.** It reports `unrecoverable` — PIN accounts with no vault row — rather than hiding them, since those are exactly the ones still needing a manual reset. It also refuses to rotate while a `PIN_PEPPER` env var is set, because the env value overrides the DB one inside `getAppSecrets()` and the rotation would otherwise silently do nothing and report success.

**A cache TTL turned out to be load-bearing.** `getAppSecrets()` caches in module scope, but each warm Edge Function instance holds its own copy — so caching indefinitely would leave `pos-staff-login`/`hr-selfservice-login` deriving with the *old* pepper after a rotation until they happened to recycle, breaking logins for an unbounded and unpredictable stretch. 60s bounds it to a minute.

**Backfill comes free from a branch that already existed.** The login functions' legacy-PIN fallback is the only place a pre-existing account's plaintext PIN is ever observable server-side, so it now writes the vault row alongside the password upgrade. No migration script, no forced resets — coverage grows as staff sign in.

**Viewing is platform-admin only**, surfaced as a new Admin → Clients → **Staff PINs** tab, deliberately *not* on `PosStaff.jsx` where Owners and POS managers would see it. Reveal is on demand only (never rendered on load), auto-masks after 30s, and writes an `audit_logs` row recording who looked at which account — never the PIN itself. Accounts with no vault row show "Not stored" with a `Tip` explaining why rather than a dead button. A forgotten PIN remains the Owner's one-click Reset PIN; this is recovery and audit, not a helpdesk shortcut.

**Stated plainly, because it should be: this is a deliberate security weakening.** PINs stop being one-way, and a compromised admin session now yields every staff PIN where before it yielded only the ability to reset them. Accepted because these are employer-assigned 4–6 digit till codes the manager already knows, and mitigated by admin-only RLS, AES-GCM under a separate key, and per-reveal auditing. **Scope is PINs only** — `create_ims_staff`/`create_hr_staff` passwords are user-chosen, 8+ chars and likely reused on other services, so they stay one-way and must never join this vault.

**Verified:** `CI=true npm run build` exits 0; 16 suites / 207 tests pass; service worker `CACHE_NAME` bumped v62 → v63. Deno still isn't installed locally, so the Edge Functions remain inspection-checked only — the deploy bundle step is their first real syntax check. **Not yet applied or deployed.** One pre-existing design-hook finding was surfaced and deliberately left alone: `ClientDrawer.js`'s plan badge uses two undocumented greys (`rgba(120,113,108,0.10)` fill, `rgba(107,114,128,0.25)` border) — real S521-class drift, but unrelated to this change and not something to fold into a security commit.

### S538 — 2026-08-12 — "Leaked Password Protection Disabled": why the toggle was the wrong fix, and what replaced it

A Security Advisor export (66 warnings) plus the "Leaked Password Protection Disabled" warning. The export's anon half turned out to be a **perfect match for the "Deliberately NOT touched" list already written into `20260810190000`** — guest QR surface, the two pre-auth pickers, the six RLS-embedded helpers — and, more usefully, confirmed S531/S532 actually landed: `record_*_pin_attempt`, `check_*_pin_lock`, both `submit_my_leave_request`/`submit_my_tada_claim` overloads and the three trigger functions are all now absent from it. Two new Supabase lint rules (0028/0029) flag the *category*, not a defect; 0029 in particular will flag essentially every RPC in this project forever, since authorization here lives inside the function bodies. Spot-checked the four `authenticated` entries that touch identity data (`client_user_emails`, `find_user_id_by_email`, `get_coworker_roster`, `get_my_client_vendors`) — all four carry their own caller check.

**One thing in that export did turn out to be wrong, on the second pass through it: the six-function "deliberately NOT touched" exemption list was four functions too long.** `20260720150000` and `20260727190000` both exempt `is_admin`, `my_client_id`, `is_hr_self_service`, `is_pos_pin_staff`, `is_ims_staff` and `is_hr_role_staff` from the anon-execute hardening, on the grounds that anon needs them for the pre-login `app_name` read. Reading the actual policy settles which ones that argument covers — `settings_select` is `client_id IS NULL OR client_id = my_client_id() OR is_admin()`, so the anon path touches exactly two. The four staff-marker helpers appear only in the RESTRICTIVE staff-isolation policies, and on `settings` those cover INSERT/UPDATE only, never SELECT; they had been carried onto the list by association, and the rolled-back-transaction test that justified it only ever covered `my_client_id()`. Re-tested the same way with all four revoked: the anon read still returns `Crest Suite`. `20260812100000` revokes them from `PUBLIC` and grants `authenticated`/`service_role`, with the anon read baked in as a commit guard plus an assertion that `is_admin`/`my_client_id` were *not* revoked — extending the list by association being the exact mistake it undoes. Not a live hole (all four are `auth.uid()`-keyed booleans that return `false` for anon); the point is that a six-function exemption where two are provably exempt invites the next person to assume the whole list was reasoned through. Drops the lint from 13 to 9. **Applied and verified live**: Guard 1 returned `Crest Suite` (the anon pre-login read still works with all four revoked), and a post-commit `has_function_privilege` sweep reads `anon=false, authenticated=true, service_role=true` on the four, with `is_admin`/`my_client_id` still `anon=true` — checked after the transaction rather than trusting Guard 2's in-transaction assertion, per the project's own rule about revokes that report success and change nothing. **The remaining 9 are permanent** — the guest QR surface, the two pre-auth pickers, and the two genuinely load-bearing helpers — so this lint can never reach zero here and is not a number to drive down.

**The password warning was real, but the dashboard toggle is not its fix — and enabling it would have broken POS.** Traced through GoTrue's source rather than the docs, which don't cover it: the HIBP check lives in `checkPasswordStrength()`, **`adminUserCreate` never calls it and `adminUserUpdate` does**. So `auth.admin.createUser()` bypasses leaked-password protection entirely, which means `register_trial` — the public signup form, the one place a human picks a real password — is precisely the path the toggle cannot reach. Meanwhile `reset_pos_pin` uses `updateUserById` with a 4–6 digit PIN, and every 4-digit string plus effectively every 6-digit numeric is in the Pwned Passwords corpus. The breakage would have been asymmetric and therefore baffling: creating a POS staff member keeps working, resetting their PIN fails on *every possible value* with "password is known to be weak".

**PINs are no longer auth passwords.** New `supabase/functions/_shared/pinPassword.ts` derives the stored password as `HMAC-SHA256(PIN_PEPPER, "<email>:<pin>")`, salted with the account's own generated `pos_email`/`hr_self_service_email` (both the create and login sides have it) and keyed with an Edge Function secret. Beyond unblocking the toggle, this closes a hole `pos-staff-login`'s own header had already described and that S531/S532 did **not** fix: because the PIN *was* the password, anyone holding an account's email could brute-force GoTrue's `/token` endpoint directly with the anon key — a path our lockout RPCs are simply not on, so moving the lockout server-side never touched it, and hiding `pos_email` only raised the cost of starting. With a pepper the password is no longer computable off-server, so that route stops existing rather than being throttled, and every path to a POS/Self-Service session now runs through the function that enforces the lockout.

**The three `createUser` password paths got their own HIBP call**, since the toggle structurally cannot cover them — k-anonymity, 5-char SHA-1 prefix, `Add-Padding`, 5s timeout, fail-open-but-logged on network failure (a HIBP outage must not take signups down). It runs in Deno, so `vercel.json`'s `connect-src` needs no new entry — that rule binds browser fetches only. This is the server-side half `weakPasswordReason()` was always documented as needing *alongside* it, not instead of it. Added to `create_ims_staff` and `create_hr_staff` as well as `register_trial`, because otherwise the two halves of one feature disagree: `reset_ims_password`/`reset_hr_password` go through `updateUserById` and *are* screened by the toggle, so a manager could set a breached password at creation and then be refused that exact password on reset.

**Both login functions carry a lazy upgrade** rather than forcing a mass PIN reset: try derived, fall back to raw PIN, and on success rewrite the account to the derived value — then re-sign-in, because a password change can revoke refresh tokens issued before it, which would otherwise hand a till a session that dies at its first refresh. The fallback counts as **one** lockout attempt, not two. Honest caveat: this does not close the direct-brute-force window by itself, it only shrinks it — the window stays open for any account that has not signed in since deploy, and the fallback block should be deleted with the stragglers force-reset once POS Staff shows none left.

**Not yet deployed or verified live.** Deno isn't installed on this machine, so the three functions could not be typechecked locally — they were verified by inspection only. Deploy order is unforgiving: `supabase secrets set PIN_PEPPER=...` **first** (`derivePinPassword()` throws fail-closed when it's missing, deliberately, rather than silently writing a raw PIN), then deploy all three functions, then smoke-test both PIN logins, and only then consider the dashboard toggle. `PIN_PEPPER` is **not recoverable and not rotatable in place** — PINs are stored nowhere, so losing it means hand-resetting every POS and Self-Service account; keep it wherever the VAPID private key lives. One adjacent thing flagged but not chased: leaked-password protection is Pro-plan-and-above.

> **Superseded by S539 before this was ever deployed.** Do not follow the deploy sequence above: there is no `supabase secrets set PIN_PEPPER` step any more. The pepper moved into the `app_secrets` table, is generated by the database, and is rotatable — apply migration `20260812110000` first instead. The "not recoverable, not rotatable" warning in this entry is exactly what prompted S539.

### S534 — 2026-08-12 — Login page: researched current practice, cross-checked the real page, fixed what it found

Asked to research login-page practice online and cross-check it against `/login`, then to fix everything the cross-check turned up. The review half was done against the running app in a real browser — computed styles, contrast ratios, hit-area rectangles and ARIA read out of the live DOM — not from reading the source, which is what caught the target-size and live-region gaps that look fine in JSX.

**A fair amount was already right and was deliberately left alone:** every field carries an explicit `autocomplete` (which is simultaneously the WCAG 2.2 SC 3.3.8 password-manager requirement and the documented defence against the Chrome autofill bleed), no paste blocking, real `<label for>` on every input, an enumeration-safe forgot-password path with a comment explaining why, `register_trial` rate-limited per-IP and globally, and auth fetches already bounded at 15s by `makeAuthTimeoutFetch`. Measured contrast came back 5.45–10.9:1 across every text tier — comfortably past 4.5:1, so nothing was changed for color.

**The password floor was three different numbers.** `Login.js` and `ResetPassword.js` both checked `< 6` (with placeholders saying "Min. 6 characters") while `register_trial` checked `< 8` — so a 7-character password passed the form, contradicted the placeholder the user had just read, and was rejected by the server. Now one exported `MIN_PASSWORD_LENGTH` in `src/utils/weakPasswords.js` (new), read by both client call sites; the Edge Function keeps its own independent copy since it can't import from the bundle, but the two now agree. 8 is NIST SP 800-63B-4's minimum (final, July 2025); the same document's 15-character recommendation is a policy call left open rather than made silently.

**Every sign-in failure said "Invalid email or password."** That generic string is correct for a credential failure — it's what stops the form being used to enumerate accounts — but it was also swallowing 429s, 5xx, and the 15s auth timeout. Telling someone who has been rate-limited that their password is wrong makes them retry harder, which is the opposite of what the limit is for. `signInErrorMessage()` now keeps one indistinguishable message across every credential outcome and separates out only the non-credential failures, where the user's correct next action genuinely differs.

**The page had zero live regions** — measured, not inferred. Errors and both confirmation panels ("reset link is on its way", "Account created!") were plain elements, so nothing was announced to a screen reader. ### S532 — 2026-08-10 — Verification pass on S531: found the PIN lockout still bypassable, and why

Not merely redundant either: `record_*_pin_attempt` is `SECURITY DEFINER` with no auth check of any kind, and its success branch is `UPDATE profiles SET *_pin_failed_attempts = 0, *_pin_locked_until = NULL WHERE id = p_staff_id` — so one anonymous POST with `p_success: true` cleared the counter for an arbitrary staff member. FROM PUBLIC` plus `service_role`-only grants on the four lockout functions.

**The root cause is a second, distinct grant trap, and it re-opened three more functions.** An EXECUTE grant is per *signature*, not per name — `REVOKE ... ### S529 — 2026-08-10 — Swept the silent 1000-row truncation across the whole app (61 sites, 42 files)

S528 found and fixed the truncation on three reads and flagged the rest as latent. This finished it. Supabase caps PostgREST at 1000 rows, and a bare `.select()` past that returns truncated data with **no error and nothing in the array to say so** — every total summed from it is then wrong, and wrong quietly.

**The worst instance was payroll, and it was worse than the flag suggested.** `hr_attendance` is one row per employee **per day**, so a period holds `staff × ~30` rows and crosses the cap at **~34 staff** — a mid-sized restaurant, not a chain. `PayrollRun.jsx` read it with no limit *and no `ORDER BY`*, then fed it straight to the payslip engine. Employees past the cutoff simply appeared to have no attendance at all, which means **daily/hourly staff get paid zero** (their pay comes *from* those rows) and **monthly staff get paid a full month with no absence deduction**. And with no sort order, *which* employees fell past the cutoff was arbitrary and could differ between two runs of the same period. ### S528 — 2026-08-10 — Stock Movements: a Sub-Recipes tab, because the ledger structurally cannot show them

Asked whether Stock Movements shows sub-recipe usage. It does not, and the reason is in the data model rather than the page: `recipe_ingredients` stores a sub-recipe as `sub_recipe_id` with **`item_id` NULL** (`Recipes.js:503-508`), so `explode()` always takes the recurse branch and only ever emits a result on reaching a real `item_id` at the bottom of the tree. The sub-recipe is a scaling step that gets discarded, and `stock_movements` has no column for the path a depletion took — so a client running a prep-heavy kitchen could see 45g of acai powder consumed but never "12.5 batches of Acai Base."

Fixed by deriving the missing layer at read time rather than writing it into the ledger. **Writing it would have been a real bug**: a sub-recipe's mirror item (`items.is_sub_recipe`) carries its own `per_uom_rate`, so extra rows would double-count the page's own "Value Depleted" KPI against the raw-item rows already there. The two tabs are the same ingredients at different grains and must never be added together — said explicitly in the UI, the Tips, Help and the IMS guide, since that is exactly the misreading the feature invites.

**`explodeRecipeIngredients` keeps its exact return shape.** Roughly eight files read it (Variance, ReorderReport, StockReport, ShrinkageReport, ClientDashboard, OwnerDashboard, `computeMonthlyReport`, `computeInventoryVariance`/`ShrinkageTrend`) and every one drives a stock or cost figure, so it became a thin wrapper over a new `explodeRecipeTree()` that returns `{ items, subRecipes }`. The two numbers the sub-recipe layer needs — output units consumed, and that ÷ the sub-recipe's own `yield_qty` — were already being computed by the walk and thrown away; they are now pushed to an out-param, which keeps the recursive leaf-item return value byte-identical. Since there's no way to reach a live authenticated session from here, that claim is backed by **11 tests** (`src/utils/recipeCost.test.js`) against a stubbed client rather than asserted: yield_pct uplift, one-level and nested sub-recipes, duplicate aggregation, an item reached both directly and through a sub-recipe, and an explicit `explodeRecipeIngredients === explodeRecipeTree(...).items` equivalence check.
